Circe’s warning

Circe foreshadows the Final Act. Mr Roe, who loves his horses, may well become one: a painted horse beneath a trooper’s saddle (see Roe’s Fate, p. 24).

The description below is echoed in the description of Roe’s fate later.

At the front of the hall is a life-sized statue of a woman. The bronze appears a slick oil black in this light. Forward-stretched arms and splayed, curled and taut fingers give the impression of the manipulation of some unseen marionette, yet her locked, straight elbows convey a rigidness not associated with nimble masters of puppetry. She wears a crown of two gasping snakes and stands on a plinth of more writhing serpents. Small cowering figures act as Atlas, holding up the entire plinth. The plaque of the statue reads: “B. Mackennal, Circe (1893), bronze.”

Homer’s Odyssey

Circe

Enchantress of Aeaea

Daughter of Helios the sun god and the nymph Perse, Circe met every visitor to her island the same way. When Odysseus landed on his long road home from Troy, she turned his crew into swine, and only his cunning won them back. Her captives were never killed. They were kept, made into something they were not.
