The Exhibit Comes Alive

The battle moves in five beats, always in this order. Move to the next beat when it suits your table: in narrative time while the players explore, in combat rounds once the shooting starts.

1The Bugle

Not long after the last person enters, once the players have had a moment to take in the scene, the spirits are forcefully summoned and the re-enactment begins.

A bugle sounds from the gully. Paint runs. Figures peel off the canvas, the wax and straw men stir, and the ground beneath you turns just a little more real.

Insight: the dead fight each other, not you. Stay out of their way and they pay you no mind. The crossfire doesn’t care: stray shots and charging horses still find investigators (p. 25).

2Mr Roe Arrives

Once the fight is properly underway, and the players have felt it, Roe lands on the viewing platform. His arrival is a hard reset. Mid-swing, fires unburn, blood unspills, the fallen are back at their posts, and the battle awaits the next bugle.

Roe lands badly. He staggers to the unfinished edge of the platform, loses his footing and tumbles down the faux terrain, rolling to a stop at the foot of the canvas.

What a reset does not undo. Investigators’ wounds, positions and pockets. They are not part of the painting, yet. Any stolen item they carry is still stolen; a fresh one forms in the scene, as it did for Charlie.

How a reset looks. Your choice: everything snaps back; the battle runs in reverse, blood flowing back into wounds and the dead walking backwards to their posts; or the scene fades to pencil and is painted back in. Whichever you pick takes a round.

If Roe Goes First

If Roe touches the canvas before the investigators, or in front of them so they all cross together, he is already next to the canvas when they land. Skip beat 2: he is already talking, and the first bugle is his fate. If Roe never comes in, skip beats 2–4.

3Roe Talks

Roe gets up and excitedly starts shouting:

“But c’mon, ghosts trapped in a painting? Can you believe it? This is a literal gold mine! We can sell licences! Mining rights! Who wouldn’t pay for all this!”

Talk of mining licences does not go down well with the diggers, nor does the prospect of eternal fights. Insight: the nearest diggers have turned to listen, and they don’t like it.

The investigators have until the next bugle to shut him up: Persuade, Fast Talk, or a hand over his mouth. Silenced, he is one more person to get out. Sound the bugle when the diggers have heard enough, or the players have had a fair chance at him.

4The Bugle Again: Roe’s Fate

If Roe is still talking at the bugle, painted hands reach out of the canvas and drag him in. No roll: he had his chance.

“No, no! You’re small business owners! This isn’t a labour issue! I love miners! Nooooooo!”

Roe becomes a beast: a painted horse, flat and wobbling, with a trooper swinging up into the saddle. The trooper’s arms stretch forward, elbows locked, fingers splayed, curled and taut around the reins, like a puppeteer working some cursed marionette. Every move Roe makes is worked by his rider.

5The Battle Finishes

The re-enactment ends a quarter of an hour after the last bugle. Much of this might be skipped once everyone is hidden and under cover. The diggers have lost, as always, and every figure stops where it fell. The sun stands a handbreadth above Mt. Warrenheip.

A foot on a ladder with something stolen still wakes its owner. Any foot on a ladder, stolen goods or not, wakes the horseman (p. 26). After a calm as long as the first, the scene resets and the bugle sounds again, skipping beats 2–4. The battle loops for as long as anyone living is inside; only when the last of them leaves does the canvas sleep.
