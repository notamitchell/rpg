Historical Epilogue

The year before the investigation:

The Argus
Melbourne, November, 1925
Success of “Opportunity Shop.”

The “Opportunity Shop” organ­ized by Lady Tallis at the Cyclorama in aid of the appeal opened on Thurs­day very success­fully. Approx­imately £300 resulted from three hours’ actual selling, but prepar­ations for those three hours occupied nearly seven weeks, and for the success of the project it is necessary that the supply of goods should be main­tained to meet the demand.

An “Opportunity Shop” or “Opp Shop”? Yes, this was the invention of the Opp Shop! If you’re interested in the origins of the Opp Shop and former showgirl, Lady Millie Tallis, see: Annear, R. (2019). Nothing New: A History of Second-Hand.

The hoop skirt: Charlie’s hiding place is a nod to Elizabeth Wilson, who by family account hid a miner beneath her hooped petticoat during the battle. See Wickham, D. (2008). “Blood, Sweat and Tears”: Women at Eureka. Journal of Australian Colonial History, 10(1), 99–114 (link on page 48).

The CSIR and the busker’s ballads: see Currie, G., & Graham, J. (1966). The origins of CSIRO: Science and the Commonwealth Government 1901–1926. CSIRO Publishing. As its blurb puts it, “In those days there were vivid contrasts in the views of scientists, politicians and men of affairs as to how science could most effectively influence the nation’s future.”

One year after the investigation:

The Argus
Melbourne, Tuesday, November 15, 1927
The Last of the Cyclorama

The work of razing the old Cyclorama in Victoria parade, Fitzroy, is now almost complete. This 50 ft. wall, 2ft. in thick­ness, was under­mined yesterday and wreathed with ropes, to which horses were attached. The photo­graph shows one of the last sections of the wall falling.

Artistic licence: The real Eureka Stockade cyclorama was much smaller than its famous siblings — about 1000 square feet of canvas, displayed in the foyer rather than filling a rotunda of its own. This adaptation scales it up to a full 360-degree canvas. Likewise, the “surviving portion” in the basement is really a photograph of one portion of the painting, printed and painted over by an artist in 1900.

Part of the Little Collins Street Cyclorama building survives to today and is heritage listed.

If you’re interested in the topic of Cycloramas, you might also want to read Canvas Documentaries: Panoramic Entertainments in Nineteenth-Century Australia and New Zealand by Mimi Colligan.

Players had a photo of the old Cyclorama on Victoria Parade, Fitzroy since Handout A. It’s visible just beyond the Library dome’s right edge: the round many-sided building where St Vincent’s Hospital is now!
